# Frameworks Reference

All frameworks below are drawn from recurring patterns across the Write With AI library. Use the index in SKILL.md to pick one; this file gives the rules.

---

## Audience clarity

### FOR WHO / SO THAT
Lock the piece in one sentence before writing:

> **FOR** [specific reader with context] **SO THAT** [specific change in their belief, behavior, or outcome].

Bad: "for marketers so they understand AI."
Good: "for solo consultants who already use ChatGPT for emails so that they stop pasting raw output and start editing for voice."

If the FOR WHO is fuzzy, the piece will be too. Refuse to draft until it is sharp.

### Category of One
Differentiation rule: position so no direct competitor occupies the same intersection. Use 7-step niche-down sequence:

1. Topic you talk about
2. Audience you serve
3. Promise you make (the change you produce)
4. Mechanism (your specific method)
5. Enemy (what you're against)
6. Format(s) you publish in
7. Voice you write in

The intersection of these seven = your category.

## Long-form structure

### 1/3/1
Universal structural template:

- **1** — One opening line that hooks (declarative, specific).
- **3** — Three middle sections (each a sub-point that supports the main idea).
- **1** — One closing line that lands the takeaway.

Variants: 1/5/1 for longer pieces, 1/3/1/3/1 for nested structure.

### SEE-I Method
For explaining one idea clearly. Four moves:

- **State** — claim in one sentence.
- **Elaborate** — explain in your own words why it's true.
- **Example** — concrete illustration.
- **Illustrate** — analogy or metaphor that makes it stick.

Use SEE-I for any piece teaching a single concept.

### Hook-Point-Action (short-form)
For LinkedIn/X posts, captions, micro-essays:

- **Hook** — line that stops the scroll.
- **Point** — the single insight, framework, or claim.
- **Action** — what the reader should do, believe, or notice next.

Three lines minimum, ten lines maximum. No padding.

### Hook-Story-Offer
For story-driven emails, sales copy, landing pages:

- **Hook** — emotional or curiosity-driven open.
- **Story** — specific narrative (real, concrete, with stakes).
- **Offer** — the thing the story makes them want.

Story must do work the offer can't do alone (proof, identification, transformation).

## Hooks

### Specificity rule
Every hook must pass: *Could this open a different article?* If yes, it's generic. Rewrite with a number, name, time, place, or contradiction.

- Generic: "Most people fail at writing online."
- Specific: "I wrote 1,247 LinkedIn posts before one hit 100k views. Here's what changed on post 1,248."

### 5-Second Rule (cold opens)
First 5 seconds (≈first sentence) must answer: *why should I keep reading right now?* Use one of:

- A specific number that breaks expectation
- A contradiction to common belief
- A confession that signals stakes
- An unfinished thought that demands resolution

### Paint The Villain
Identify the enemy your reader already resents (a belief, system, gatekeeper, common practice). Open by naming it. Reader nods → they're in.

Examples of villains: "the writing advice everyone gives," "the LinkedIn-guru playbook," "the way schools taught us to outline."

### How-To 4-part headline formula
Structure: `How To [desired outcome] [in time/with constraint] [without pain] [for specific reader]`

> "How To Write A Newsletter In 30 Minutes Without Burning Out For Founders Who Hate Marketing."

Drop one or two slots if it reads cleaner. All four = max specificity.

### Hormozi clarity test
Strip cleverness. The headline should announce the value plainly. If a reader has to decode it, they scroll. Clear > clever.

## Storytelling

### Epiphany Bridge
Five-beat structure for persuasive personal story:

1. **Backstory** — who you were before.
2. **Wall** — what you ran into that didn't work.
3. **Epiphany** — the realization that broke the wall.
4. **Plan** — what you did differently after.
5. **Result** — what changed (concrete, measurable).

Use to install one new belief in the reader.

### 5-Sentence Story
Compressed narrative for social posts:

1. Setup (specific setting + character)
2. Conflict (what went wrong)
3. Turning point (what shifted)
4. Resolution (what happened)
5. Lesson (what it means for the reader)

### Customer pain categories (7)
Most resonant content speaks to one of these:

1. Loss of status
2. Loss of money
3. Loss of time
4. Loss of identity / self-image
5. Fear of being wrong / looking stupid
6. Fear of being left behind
7. Frustration with a system that should work but doesn't

Pick one before writing. Mention the specific pain in the hook or first paragraph.

### Joke Mine
Connect personal story → offer using comedic structure. Setup is the mundane detail; punchline is the unexpected business insight. Humor disarms; insight lands.

## Voice

### Five voice types
1. **Authoritative** — declarative, data-backed, no hedging.
2. **Conversational** — second person, contractions, asks questions.
3. **Contrarian** — opens by disagreeing with consensus.
4. **Vulnerable** — admits failure, names internal struggle.
5. **Instructive** — step-by-step, teacher voice, examples.

Pick a primary, blend a secondary. Don't mix more than two — the piece loses signal.

### Tone Grid (avoid neutral zone)
Plot tone on two axes:

- **Energy:** calm ↔ intense
- **Stance:** earnest ↔ playful

Neutral-zone writing sits in the dead middle of both axes — it offends no one and moves no one. Force the piece toward a corner.

### Hemingway pass
After draft: cut every adverb, replace passive with active, break compound sentences, prefer concrete nouns over abstractions, end paragraphs on the strongest word.

## Idea generation

### Idea Machine
One prompt the user runs daily: feed AI three inputs — *(1) audience, (2) recent reader question, (3) personal experience this week*. Output: 5 angles. Pick one. Write.

### ABC Brainstorm
Stuck? Run the alphabet. For each letter A–Z, write one word related to the topic. Forces lateral connections; breaks repetitive thinking.

### 1 Topic, 100 Ideas
Pick one topic. Generate 100 angles using these multipliers: 10 questions × 10 frames (how-to, story, contrarian, list, case study, mistake, framework, prediction, retrospective, comparison). 10 × 10 = 100.

### 1-5-50 Method
From one topic, generate 5 hooks, then expand each into 10 variations = 50 distinct openers. Test them.

### Limiting Beliefs Post
Powerful niche-establishing post type. List 5–10 beliefs your audience holds that hold them back. For each: name it, explain why it persists, replace it.

## Repurposing

### Long → Short
Take one long-form piece. Extract:
- 1 thread / carousel (top 5 points)
- 5 quote-card lines (single most-quotable sentences)
- 3 short-form posts (one per sub-point)
- 1 email (story angle from the piece)

### Short → Long
Take one short-form post that performed. Expand:
- Original post becomes the thesis
- Each sentence becomes a sub-section
- Add one example + one counter-example per section
- Frame the whole thing in 1/3/1

### 1 → 10 (article to social)
One article → ten social posts. Mine for:
- Strongest single sentence × 3 (post each as standalone)
- Counter-intuitive claim × 1
- Personal anecdote × 1
- Framework summary × 1
- List of N × 1
- Question that invites reply × 1
- Common mistake call-out × 1
- Direct CTA back to the article × 1

### Video → Article
Transcript → outline → article. Keep the speaker's strongest phrasing verbatim; restructure for reading rhythm, not listening rhythm.

## Editing

### Kill weak words
Default delete list: *just, really, basically, actually, simply, very, that, in order to, the fact that, it is important to note, due to the fact*. Each one signals a writer who didn't trust the sentence.

### Simplify
- One idea per sentence.
- Cut the second half of any sentence that uses "and" to staple two ideas together.
- Replace 3-syllable words with 1-syllable when meaning is identical.

### Flow check (paragraphs)
Read first sentence of each paragraph in order. They should form a coherent outline. If not, the paragraphs aren't carrying the argument — restructure before polishing.

### The "so what" test
After every paragraph, ask: *so what?* If the answer isn't obvious to the reader, either cut the paragraph or add the so-what explicitly.

### Surgical edits (preserve voice)
When asking AI to revise a passage, default to wholesale rewrites and you will get sanded-off, generic-correct prose. The voice goes flat one paragraph at a time. Constrain the edit explicitly:

> "Only change this part and do not change anything else. Stay close to the original text, its lexical and syntactic choices."

Use this constraint every time AI touches existing prose. Variants:

- For a single sentence: *"Tighten this sentence. Keep my word choices, my sentence length, and my rhythm. Only fix what's broken."*
- For grammar pass: *"Correct grammar and typos only. Do not rephrase. Do not 'improve' anything."*
- For a section: *"Edit only this section. Match the voice of the rest of the piece. Do not introduce vocabulary or sentence patterns I haven't used elsewhere."*

The principle: AI's default is regression to corporate-blog mean. Force it to stay inside the writer's grain.

## AI Workflow

These are post-draft tools, not drafting aids. Use after the piece is structurally done but before publish. Especially valuable for opinion essays, research-heavy posts, anything making factual claims.

### Claim validation table
Run after the draft is structurally complete. Surface every checkable claim and grade it.

> "Extract at least 10 claims from the text below that could be factually or logically wrong. For each claim, search the web to verify it, then return a table with: the claim, a score (✅ accurate / ⚠️ partial / ❌ inaccurate / 🔍 unverifiable), a one-line justification, and a reference URL."

Then walk the table. Anything ⚠️ / ❌ / 🔍 needs revision before publish — soften the claim, find a real source, or cut.

### Counter-claim search (anti-confirmation-bias)
Most drafts only argue one direction. Force the other direction into view.

> "For every claim in the table above, find a counter-claim. Search the web for it, provide a reference URL, and explain in one sentence why this counter-claim deserves serious consideration."

You won't necessarily change the piece. But you will know what the smart reader is going to throw back at you, and can pre-empt the strongest objections in the text itself.

### Source markers
While drafting, mark unverified claims and missing sources inline as `(search X)` or `(add link to Y)`. Don't break flow to chase a citation. Resolve all markers in a single pass before publish.

### Manual link verification
After AI inserts hyperlinks (citations, references, examples), open every link before publishing. AI will hallucinate plausible-looking URLs. The cost of one fake citation surviving to publish is much higher than the time it takes to click each link.

### When to skip the AI Workflow
Personal essays, narrative posts, reflections — pieces that aren't making external factual claims — don't need the validation/counter-claim passes. They still benefit from surgical-edit constraints.
