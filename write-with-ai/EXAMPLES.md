# Examples

Before/after specimens. Each shows a framework applied. Use these as reference shapes, not templates to copy verbatim.

---

## Example 1 — Sharpening with FOR WHO / SO THAT

**User request:** "Write me a LinkedIn post about productivity."

**Bad response (don't do this):** Drafts a generic post with bullet points about time-blocking.

**Good response:** Refuse to draft. Ask:
> Before I write: who exactly is the reader — a first-time founder drowning in Slack, a senior IC who already runs deep-work blocks, a designer freelancing solo? And what one belief should change for them after reading? "Productivity" is a category, not a piece. Give me one of those and I'll have a piece worth writing.

After user clarifies "first-time founder, 6 months in, working 12-hour days, believes more hours = more output":

**Sharpened sentence (FOR WHO / SO THAT):**
> FOR first-time founders six months in who still believe more hours equals more output SO THAT they cut to a 6-hour rule and notice their throughput goes up, not down.

Now there is a piece to write.

## Example 2 — Hook rewrite (specificity rule)

**Generic:**
> Most people struggle with writer's block.

**Failure mode:** Could open any of 50,000 articles. Reader scrolls.

**Specific rewrites (pick one):**
- "I went 47 days without publishing. The fix wasn't discipline — it was a 12-minute walk."
- "Writer's block is a category error. You don't have block. You have a sentence you don't believe yet."
- "Stephen King writes 2,000 words a day. I tried it for one week and quit on day 3 — here's what broke."

Each one has a number, a contradiction, or a stake. Each could only open *this* piece.

## Example 3 — Hook-Point-Action (LinkedIn post)

**Topic:** Designers who undercharge.

```
For 3 years I charged $50/hr and called it "fair."

Then a client said: "Your work is worth more. I'm worried you'll quit before I can hire you full-time."

The price isn't the problem. The story you tell yourself about the price is.

If you're undercharging, write down what you'd say to a junior designer charging your rate. Then say it to yourself.
```

- **Hook** = confession + specific dollar figure
- **Point** = reframe (price vs. self-story)
- **Action** = one concrete exercise

No padding. No "in conclusion." No "let me know in the comments."

## Example 4 — 1/3/1 long-form skeleton

**Piece:** "Why most newsletters die at 200 subscribers."

```
1 — Hook
"200 is the cliff. Most newsletters die there. Here's why — and the one thing that gets you past it."

3 — Body
Section A: What changes at 200 (you run out of friends; cold readers replace warm ones).
Section B: Why most writers fail to notice (they keep writing for the people who already loved them).
Section C: The shift — write the next post for the reader you haven't met yet.

1 — Land
"The first 200 readers are a favor. The next 2,000 are a craft. Stop writing thank-you notes — start writing for strangers."
```

Three middle sections each carry one sub-point. The close echoes the open without repeating it.

## Example 5 — Paint The Villain

**Topic:** "AI writing advice is mostly noise."

**Open:**
> "Use ChatGPT to 10x your writing." Every LinkedIn guru is selling the same prompt pack. Most of it produces sludge. The fix isn't a better prompt — it's deciding what you actually want to say before you open the chat window.

The villain (lazy AI advice) is named in the first sentence. Reader who already resents that genre nods → keeps reading.

## Example 6 — Epiphany Bridge (story for sales email)

```
Backstory: Two years ago I was a freelance writer billing $35/hr.

Wall: I tried raising rates 6 times. Lost 6 clients. Went back down each time.

Epiphany: A mentor said "you're not raising rates — you're asking permission. Stop asking."

Plan: Next client conversation, I named $150/hr as the starting point. Didn't justify. Didn't apologize.

Result: They said yes. I'd been the only one who didn't believe the number.
```

One belief installed: *the price problem is a permission problem*. The piece earns the right to make an offer next.

## Example 7 — Edit by deletion

**Before (89 words):**
> In today's fast-paced world of content creation, it's really important to actually understand that writing is, basically, a skill that takes time and effort to develop. Many people simply assume that they can just sit down and start producing high-quality content right away, but the fact of the matter is that, in order to truly excel, you need to put in the work. The key is to just keep showing up every single day, no matter what.

**After (28 words):**
> Writing takes time. Most people sit down expecting quality on day one. The work is showing up on day 200 — when no one is watching and the post is mid.

Cuts: every "really," "actually," "basically," "just," "simply." Removes throat-clearing ("In today's fast-paced world"). Replaces vague claim ("put in the work") with a specific image ("day 200, no one watching"). Lands harder in 31% of the words.

## Example 8 — Repurposing 1 → 10

Source: a 1,200-word essay titled *"The 200-subscriber cliff."*

Ten outputs:
1. Quote-card line: "The first 200 readers are a favor. The next 2,000 are a craft."
2. Quote-card line: "200 is the cliff."
3. Counter-intuitive post: "Stop writing thank-you notes. Start writing for strangers."
4. Personal anecdote post: my own 200 → 200 plateau, what broke me out.
5. Framework summary post: 3 shifts that get you past the cliff.
6. List post: 7 things that change at 200 subscribers.
7. Reply-bait question: "What number was *your* cliff?"
8. Mistake call-out: "The #1 reason writers quit at 200 — and it's not what you think."
9. Subject line for newsletter: "200 (a number that killed more newsletters than any other)."
10. CTA back to article: "I wrote about this here →"

One essay → ten distinct distribution shapes. None of them are the essay.

## Example 9 — Voice switch demo

**Same point, five voices:**

- **Authoritative:** "Newsletters die at 200 subscribers. The fix is one decision: write for the reader you haven't met."
- **Conversational:** "Ever notice how newsletters stall around 200? Yeah. There's a reason — and you're probably making it worse."
- **Contrarian:** "Everyone says 'just keep writing.' That's how newsletters die at 200. The advice is wrong."
- **Vulnerable:** "I plateaued at 207 subscribers for 11 weeks. I almost quit. Here's what broke me out."
- **Instructive:** "If your newsletter is stuck at 200, do this in order: (1) audit your last 10 posts, (2) circle who they were written for, (3) rewrite the next one for someone outside that circle."

Same point. Five different doors. Pick the one that matches who *you* sound like — not who you wish you sounded like.

## Example 10 — Tone Grid placement

A draft that reads:
> "Writing online is a journey. Be patient, stay consistent, and trust the process."

Plot it: low energy, earnest. Right in the dead middle. No corner. No edge.

Push to a corner:
- **Intense + earnest:** "Writing online is brutal for the first 18 months. Be ready or don't start."
- **Calm + playful:** "Writing online is mostly typing into the void. The void eventually types back. Sometimes."
- **Intense + playful:** "Most online writers quit before their first viral post. Don't be most online writers."

Each version has a position. The original had none.
